02. Scheduling in Batch Systems

Batch processing represents the foundational operational paradigm of mainframe computing and modern High-Performance Computing (HPC) clusters. In a batch environment, jobs are submitted non-interactively to a spooling queue. Because no human user sits at a terminal waiting for immediate keystroke feedback, the primary optimization objectives shift decisively:

  • Maximizing Throughput (X): Completing the maximum number of computational tasks per hour.
  • Minimizing Turnaround Time (Tturnaround): Delivering finished outputs as quickly as possible.
  • Maximizing CPU Utilization (U): Ensuring expensive silicon execution units remain saturated near 100% capacity.

1. First-Come, First-Served (FCFS)

The simplest and most intuitive scheduling discipline is First-Come, First-Served (FCFS). Tasks are assigned the processor strictly in the chronological order of their arrival in the Ready queue.

Mechanics & Implementation

The operating system manages the Ready list as a standard FIFO (First-In, First-Out) queue. When a job enters the Ready state, its Process Control Block (PCB) is linked to the tail of the queue. The dispatcher repeatedly pulls the task from the queue head, loads its register state, and allows it to run.

Non-Preemptive Invariant: FCFS is strictly non-preemptive. Once a process acquires a CPU core, it retains exclusive ownership until it voluntarily relinquishes control—either by terminating (exit()) or blocking on an I/O request (read(), write()).

  • Implementation Complexity: Trivially minimal. Enqueue and dequeue operations operate in O(1) time with zero algorithmic overhead.
  • Starvation Immunity: Because the arrival order is monotonic and immutable, every queued process will eventually reach the head of the queue. Starvation is impossible under finite job lengths.

The Convoy Effect

Despite its simplicity, FCFS suffers from a severe performance vulnerability known as the convoy effect.

Consider a workload containing one massive, compute-bound process (Process A requiring 24 ms of continuous CPU execution) followed by three lightweight, I/O-bound processes (Processes B, C, and D, each requiring only 3 ms of computation before issuing disk writes).

2. Shortest Job First (SJF)

To eliminate the convoy effect and minimize average waiting time, batch architectures turn to Shortest Job First (SJF). When the CPU becomes free, the scheduler inspects the Ready queue and selects the job with the smallest required CPU burst duration.

Mathematical Proof of SJF Turnaround Optimality

Theorem: When all jobs arrive simultaneously at time T = 0, non-preemptive Shortest Job First is provably optimal: it yields the minimum possible average waiting time and minimum average turnaround time among all non-preemptive algorithms.

Formal Proof:
Consider n jobs arriving at T = 0 with execution burst durations t1, t2, ..., tn.
Suppose the scheduler executes these jobs in arbitrary permutation π = (π1, π2, ..., πn).

The waiting time for the first job executed (π1) is 0.
The waiting time for the second job (π2) is tπ1.
The waiting time for the third job (π3) is tπ1 + tπ2.
In general, the waiting time for the k-th job is the sum of all preceding burst lengths: 
Twait(πk) = ∑j=1k−1 tπj
Summing the waiting times across all n jobs yields the total system waiting time: 
Total Twait = (n − 1)tπ1 + (n − 2)tπ2 + (n − 3)tπ3 + ... + 1·tπn−1 + 0·tπn
Notice that the burst time of the first job (tπ1) is added into the waiting time of all (n − 1) remaining jobs. The burst time of the second job is added into (n − 2) jobs, and so on.

To minimize this linear combination ∑i=1n (n − i)tπi, coefficients (n − i) strictly decrease as i increases. By the Rearrangement Inequality, the sum is minimized if and only if the burst times are ordered in monotonically non-decreasing order: 
tπ1 ≤ tπ2 ≤ tπ3 ≤ ... ≤ tπn
Since Average Turnaround Time is simply Average Waiting Time plus the constant mean burst time, minimizing total waiting time simultaneously minimizes average turnaround time. Q.E.D.

Revisiting the Convoy Workload with SJF

Applying non-preemptive SJF to the earlier workload (Jobs B, C, D requiring 3 ms; Job A requiring 24 ms, all available at T = 0):

  • Execution Order: B → C → D → A
  • Waiting Times: Twait(B) = 0 ms, Twait(C) = 3 ms, Twait(D) = 6 ms, Twait(A) = 9 ms.
  • Average Waiting Time: (0 + 3 + 6 + 9) / 4 = 4.5 ms (vs. 20.25 ms under FCFS — a 78% reduction).
  • Average Turnaround Time: (3 + 6 + 9 + 33) / 4 = 12.75 ms (vs. 28.5 ms under FCFS — a 55% reduction).

The Arrival Timing Dilemma & Starvation

SJF's mathematical optimality holds strictly only when all candidate jobs arrive simultaneously.

  • Arrival Order Vulnerability: If Job A (24 ms) arrives at T = 0, and Jobs B, C, and D arrive at T = 1 ms, non-preemptive SJF cannot preempt Job A. The system degenerates into FCFS, and the convoy effect re-emerges.
  • Starvation (Indefinite Postponement): If short jobs arrive in a steady, continuous stream, a long-running compute job will sit in the Ready queue indefinitely, starved of CPU cycles.

3. Shortest Remaining Time Next (SRTN)

To prevent short jobs from being trapped when they arrive immediately after a long job begins running, operating systems introduce preemption. Shortest Remaining Time Next (SRTN) is the preemptive variant of Shortest Job First.

Preemptive Evaluation Logic

Whenever a new process enters the Ready queue, the scheduler does not wait for the currently executing process to finish. Instead, it compares the expected remaining execution time of the currently running task against the full burst duration of the newly arrived task:

  • If the newly arrived process requires less execution time than the current task has remaining, the running task is immediately preempted.
  • The preempted task's register state is preserved in its PCB/trap frame, its state is moved back to TASK_RUNNING (in the Ready list), and the dispatcher switches the CPU core to the new task.
  • If the new process requires more time than remains on the current task, the running process continues uninterrupted.

4. Predicting Future Bursts: Exponential Smoothing

The fatal practical limitation of both SJF and SRTN in general-purpose operating systems is obvious: the scheduler cannot know how long a process will execute before it runs. A process may loop three times, read a file, or loop ten million times depending on user input.

To approximate SJF/SRTN, the operating system estimates the next CPU burst duration based on the process's past measured behavior using an exponential moving average (exponential smoothing).

Mathematical Recurrence Formulation

Let tn be the length of the n-th actual, measured CPU burst. Let τn be the predicted value for the n-th burst. The prediction for the upcoming (n + 1)-th burst is calculated recursively as: 

τn+1 = αtn + (1 − α)τn

where α (0 ≤ α ≤ 1) is a configurable smoothing parameter that controls the weighting:

  • α = 0 (τn+1 = τn): Recent history has zero influence. The prediction remains frozen at its initial baseline τ0.
  • α = 1 (τn+1 = tn): Past history has zero influence. The next burst is assumed to exactly equal the most recent single burst. Highly vulnerable to temporary anomalies.
  • α = 0.5 (Balanced Exponential Decay): Equal weight is assigned to the immediate past burst and the historical aggregate. This is the canonical default in production systems.

Expanding the Recurrence Relation

Expanding the formula reveals how historical burst observations decay geometrically over time: 

τn+1 = αtn + (1 − α)αtn−1 + (1 − α)2αtn−2 + ... + (1 − α)jαtn−j + ... + (1 − α)n+1τ0

Because (1 − α) < 1, the term (1 − α)j shrinks exponentially as j increases. Older burst lengths rapidly lose their mathematical weight. If a process historically performed long 100 ms calculations but recently transitions into an interactive I/O phase (producing 2 ms bursts), τ adjusts downward within 3 to 4 scheduling cycles, dynamically reclassifying the task as short.
