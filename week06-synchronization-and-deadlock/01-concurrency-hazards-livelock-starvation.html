Module 01 • COSC240

Concurrency Hazards: Livelock, Starvation, and Priority Inversion

Examine the non-deadlock failure modes of concurrency. Explore how active state oscillations induce livelock, how unfair scheduler heuristics cause thread starvation, and how Priority Inversion nearly destroyed the Mars Pathfinder mission.

1. The Spectrum of Concurrency Hazards

When designing concurrent multithreaded systems, preventing race conditions via mutexes and semaphores is only the first hurdle. Even when mutual exclusion is correctly implemented, thread interactions can lead to systemic operational failures where tasks make zero forward progress.

  • Deadlock: Threads are blocked indefinitely, waiting for resources held by each other in a closed circular chain.
  • Livelock: Threads are actively executing instructions and responding to state changes, but make zero forward progress. The system oscillates endlessly between conflicting states without blocking (e.g., two people trying to pass each other in a narrow hallway, continuously stepping to the same side in unison).
  • Starvation: A scheduling injustice where a thread is perpetually denied access to a CPU or resource because higher-priority threads dominate the queue. Unlike deadlock, the starved thread is ready and willing to run, but the scheduler never selects it.

2. Priority Inversion and The Mars Pathfinder Anomaly

Priority inversion occurs when a high-priority thread is indirectly preempted by a medium-priority thread, violating the core promise of priority-based preemptive scheduling.

The most famous engineering illustration of priority inversion occurred in July 1997 aboard the Mars Pathfinder spacecraft shortly after landing on the Martian surface.

  • The Setup: The spacecraft ran VxWorks real-time OS. A low-priority meteorological task (L) held a mutex protecting shared communications memory. A high-priority telemetry communications task (H) needed the mutex to transmit science data.
  • The Inversion Mechanism: While L held the mutex, a medium-priority scientific data gathering task (M) became ready to run. Because M had higher priority than L, the scheduler preempted L. M executed continuously, starving L of CPU cycles. Consequently, L could never release the mutex.
  • The Deadlock Collision: High-priority task H attempted to acquire the mutex, blocking indefinitely on L. Because H was blocked and L was starved by M, H never executed.
  • The Failure Consequence: The spacecraft's onboard watchdog timer noticed that the high-priority communications task H had failed to check in for a prolonged interval, assuming a fatal software hang, and triggered a hard system reset.
The Solution — Priority Inheritance Protocol (PIP):
To solve the Pathfinder anomaly, NASA engineers activated a VxWorks flag implementing the Priority Inheritance Protocol:
  • When a high-priority thread blocks on a mutex held by a low-priority thread, the low-priority thread temporarily inherits the priority of the highest-priority thread waiting for its lock.
  • In the Pathfinder scenario, L instantly inherits H's high priority, preventing medium-priority task M from preempting it. L finishes its critical section rapidly, releases the mutex, and H acquires it immediately.
